## Paper 1: Which Group Is Better? The Development of Statistical Reasoning in Elementary School Children

**Main findings**

• Nearly everyone got it right when the averages were far apart. It got harder when they were close, and hardest when the groups were different sizes.
• About a third of 3rd graders and two thirds of 6th graders used proportional reasoning.
• 6th graders did better overall, but very few kids at either age reasoned in a fully statistical way.
• Most kids were somewhere in the middle ("proto-statistical"), using part of the right idea.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Kids already have this halfway reasoning without being taught, so it's a good starting point for DataScope activities.
• We could design tasks that push that thinking further and try them with a wider mix of kids.

**Reference**

Gal, I., Rothschild, K., & Wagner, D. A. (1989). Which Group Is Better? The Development of Statistical Reasoning in Elementary School Children. Paper presented at the SRCD meeting, Kansas City, MO.

## Paper 4: Emergent Statistical Modeling: Elementary School Students' Transitioning from Modeling of Data to Modeling for Statistical Reasoning

**Main findings**

• The two kids started with a straight line ("longer arms = more jumps") and ignored anyone who didn't fit.
• After spotting exceptions they softened it to "longer arms help you jump more".
• Once they ran lots of random samples, they kept their drawing on screen and used it to check if each new sample looked normal.
• So the drawing went from describing one dataset to being a tool for thinking. No formal maths needed.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Gives us a lesson flow we can borrow: explore real data, sketch a rough model, simulate samples, compare.
• We could try it with more kids using lighter tools, and see if it carries over to a new dataset.

**Reference**

Dvir, M. (2026). Emergent statistical modeling: elementary school students' transitioning from modeling of data to modeling for statistical reasoning. Educational Studies in Mathematics, 123(2), 293–313.

## Paper 5: What Shapes Statistical and Data Literacy Research in K-12 STEM Education? A Systematic Review

**Main findings**

• 83 studies reviewed, mostly in maths and lower secondary school.
• Most measured thinking skills, and few looked at attitudes or at doing actual data work.
• What worked: real-world data, kids collecting their own data, inquiry cycles (PPDAC), digital tools like TinkerPlots, games, and cross-subject teaching.
• Experts want whole investigations instead of isolated skills, critical data literacy from primary school, and better teacher training.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Strong support for DataScope's approach: real data, kids' own data, inquiry and digital tools.
• Add game elements and "can we trust this data?" activities.
• Measure interest and confidence, not just scores, when testing with kids.

**Reference**

Friedrich, A., Schreiter, S., Vogel, M., Becker-Genschow, S., Brünken, R., Kuhn, J., Lehmann, J., & Malone, S. (2024). What shapes statistical and data literacy research in K-12 STEM education? A systematic review of metrics and instructional strategies. International Journal of STEM Education, 11, 58. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40594-024-00517-z

## Paper 6: Elementary School Teachers' Understanding of the Mean and Median

**Main findings**

• All 3 teachers could calculate the mean and median.
• 2 of the 3 struggled to explain what the numbers mean. One just repeated the steps, another mixed the two up.
• In the catapult task most of them used spread to pick the better catapult, so they understood more than their definitions showed.
• All 3 thought you could read the exact mean/median off a histogram, which you can't.

**How it helps DataScope**

• We can't assume teachers fully get mean and median either, so DataScope's teacher notes should explain the ideas, not just the steps.
• The catapult-style task is worth trying with a bigger group to see if it builds real understanding.

**Reference**

Jacobbe, T. (2012). Elementary School Teachers' Understanding of the Mean and Median. International Journal of Science and Mathematics Education, 10, 1143–1161.

## Paper 7: Preservice Elementary Teachers' Conceptual and Procedural Knowledge of Mean, Median, and Mode

**Main findings**

• Most answers just listed how to work out each measure and didn't link them together.
• Only 13 of 46 said all three describe the centre or typical value, and only 3 talked about when one is a better choice than another.
• Some used the phrase "measures of central tendency" without really knowing what it meant.
• Definitions had holes: only 3 mentioned ordering the data AND handling an even number of values for the median, and only 2 said the mode can be a category, not just a number.
• Their thinking looked a lot like what earlier studies found in school pupils.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Gives us a ready-made list of common mistakes to build tasks around (e.g. the median needs ordering, the mode works for categories too).
• The 4 levels could work as a simple rubric for how deep a child's understanding is.
• We could build an activity that moves learners from procedures to real understanding and use these levels to check if it worked.

**Reference**

Groth, R. E., & Bergner, J. A. (2006). Preservice Elementary Teachers' Conceptual and Procedural Knowledge of Mean, Median, and Mode. Mathematical Thinking and Learning, 8(1), 37–63.

## Paper 8: Opportunities to Learn Mean, Median, and Mode Afforded by Textbook Tasks

**Main findings**

• Looked at 1,392 tasks from 17 Swedish textbooks for ages 10–13.
• Mean tasks usually had a real-life context (about 72%), but mode tasks mostly didn't (only 30%).
• Most tasks were just "do the calculation".
• The deeper properties were mostly covered for the mean (73%). The median (18%) and mode (9%) barely got a look in.
• Basically, textbooks focus on working out the mean and don't do much to explain the median and mode.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Clear gap to fill: the median and mode need more real-life, meaningful tasks.
• We could make those tasks and test them with real students, which this paper didn't do.

**Reference**

Landtblom, K. (2023). Opportunities to Learn Mean, Median, and Mode Afforded by Textbook Tasks. Statistics Education Research Journal, 22(3), Article 6.
https://doi.org/10.52041/serj.v22i3.655

## Paper 9: Mean, Median, and Mode in School Years 4–6

**Main findings**

• Everyone mostly showed "how to calculate it" knowledge, not "what it means".
• Students found the mode easiest and the median hardest to explain, and said the median only exists in school.
• Trainee teachers found the mode hardest, but teachers found it easiest.
• All groups thought the mean was the most useful.
• In textbooks, 62% of tasks were about the mean, 19% the median and 18% the mode, and most just follow the definition (67–81%).
• Basically, the weak understanding people show matches what the textbooks give them.

**How it helps DataScope**

• The median and mode need real contexts so kids don't see them as "school-only".
• Reverse calculation and debug tasks are a good way to link the calculation to the meaning.
• We should be careful with words like "middle", since they shape how kids understand the measures.
• We could add tasks that ask kids to check if their answer makes sense in context.

**Reference**

Landtblom, K. (year not given in the pasted text). Mean, Median, and Mode in School Years 4–6. Compilation thesis (kappa + 5 papers), Stockholm University.
Link: not added yet

## Paper 10: Identifying Students' Specific Misconceptions in Learning Probability

**Main findings**

• 177 Year 10–11 students in Brunei did a two-tier probability questionnaire, and 17 were interviewed.
• Most mistakes were on "least likely sequence" questions (up to 37.9%), and the fewest on straight calculation.
• In interviews, the most common misconception was Beliefs (luck, God, "my own experience"), followed by Representativeness (e.g. "four heads in a row is rare").
• Some wrong answers were just careless or used the wrong method, not real misconceptions.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Good list of misconceptions to design DataScope activities around.
• The "answer + why" format is a quick way to check if a kid really gets it.
• Even older students had these, so tackling them early makes sense.

**Reference**

Ang, L. H., & Shahrill, M. (2014). Identifying students' specific misconceptions in learning probability. International Journal of Probability and Statistics, 3(2), 23–29. https://doi.org/10.5923/j.ijps.20140302.01

## Paper 11: The Challenges of Teaching Probability in School (Graham A. Jones)

**Main findings**

• It's a discussion paper, no study or data.
• Probability moved from high school counting problems to big ideas that start in elementary school.
• Variation and randomness are now seen as the starting point, since kids (and even older students) have shaky ideas about them.
• Good teaching: let kids predict, then compare with real data and models, then refine.
• Start from real data and frequencies instead of dice and counting, and keep linking probability to statistics.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Supports a data-first approach to probability, which fits DataScope.
• Predict → compare with data → refine could be our activity flow.
• Tasks should have purpose and utility for kids.
• We could actually test these ideas with real kids.

**Reference**

Jones, G. A. (year not given in the PDF). The challenges of teaching probability in school. Griffith University, School of Education and the Arts.

## Paper 12: Correcting Students' Misconceptions about Probability

**Main findings**

• Activities were designed to make students see their guesses were wrong (e.g. predicting coin sequences, then tossing hundreds of times).
• Classes with trained instructors had fewer students with representativeness and outcome orientation misconceptions than the control group.
• Classes with untrained instructors showed no improvement.
• "Everything is equally likely" was the hardest to fix, and many students still had misconceptions at the end.

**How it helps DataScope**

• The predict → collect data → compare activity is easy to adapt for kids.
• Add activities with unequal chances.
• Teachers need training, not just materials.

**Reference**

Khazanov, L., & Prado, L. (2010). Correcting students' misconceptions about probability in an introductory college statistics course. Adults Learning Mathematics – An International Journal, 5(1), 23–35.
Link: not added yet

## Paper 13: An Investigation of Fifth Grade Students' Conceptual Understanding of Probability

**Main findings**

• Both groups started at the same level, but after the teaching the activity group scored clearly higher on all three concepts (big effect sizes).
• Before the teaching, kids leaned on intuition, luck, "who spins it" and writing answers as %.
• Some kids thought every outcome is equally likely because of how a normal dice works. This dropped in the activity group but not in the normal class.
• Knowing the sample space was key for everything else.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Backs up our plan to let kids experiment and discuss in small groups.
• Their 0–5 rubric and list of common mistakes could help us score and design tasks.
• Start probability activities with listing all possible outcomes.

**Reference**

Gürbüz, R., Çatlıoğlu, H., Birgin, O., & Erdem, E. (2010). An Investigation of Fifth Grade Students' Conceptual Development of Probability through Activity Based Instruction: A Quasi-Experimental Study. Educational Sciences: Theory & Practice, 10(2), 1053–1068.

## Paper 16: Young Children's Probabilistic and Statistical Reasoning in the Context of Informal Statistical Inference

**Main findings**

• 28 kindergarten kids drew coloured balls from a box and recorded each one with coloured cards.
• Most knew they couldn't draw a colour that wasn't in the box, but only 10 explained that yellow came up most because there were more yellow balls.
• Only 5 made a proper bar graph, but 16 made a pie chart (they called the sectors "pizza slices").
• Kids kept rearranging their cards, and some expected patterns or "magic".

**How it helps DataScope**

• Great model for young users: collect data one by one, then build the chart yourself.
• Give extra help with bar graphs and make it easy to rearrange.
• Show what's in the box next to the results and ask "why?"

**Reference**

Kurt, G. (2023). Young children's probabilistic and statistical reasoning in the context of informal inferential reasoning. Statistics Education Research Journal, 22(2), Article 4. https://doi.org/10.52041/serj.v22i2.434

## Paper 17: A Framework for Thinking about Informal Statistical Inference (Makar & Rubin)

**Main findings**

• Three principles: make a claim beyond your data, use data as evidence, and say how sure you are.
• A Year 5 survey unit failed because it had no clear question, a boring topic and yes/no data.
• Year 4 kids linked conclusions to data better when asked "what does this graph tell me?"
• Year 3 kids first thought the class next door would be identical, but after comparing classes they made predictions like "maybe 15 or 16 cm".

**How it helps DataScope**

• Core design lens for DataScope: beyond the data + evidence + "how sure are you?"
• Start every activity with a clear question kids care about.
• Compare our data with another group, then predict a new one.

**Reference**

Makar, K., & Rubin, A. (2009). A framework for thinking about informal statistical inference. Statistics Education Research Journal, 8(1), 82–105.

## Paper 18: Investigating Statistical Predictions with First Graders in Greece

**Main findings**

• 26 first graders predicted next weekend's numbers from a small table.
• Before the lessons many just guessed or added numbers up. After, most picked a number within the range of the data (e.g. Q1: 11% to 78%).
• Predicting was harder when the numbers were spread out (11, 16, 14).
• Only kids who did the homework with their parents looked at the data as a whole (28% on Q4).

**How it helps DataScope**

• Their list of answer types can help us spot where a kid's thinking is.
• "Predict next week from a table" tasks are easy to reuse.
• Start with narrow-range data, then spread it out.
• Parent-and-child activities could be worth adding.

**Reference**

Michalopoulou, A., & Kafoussi, S. (2024). Investigating statistical predictions with first graders in Greece. International Electronic Journal of Mathematics Education, 19(2), em0771. https://doi.org/10.29333/iejme/14247

## Paper 19: Exploring Informal Statistical Inference in Early Statistics: A Learning Trajectory for Third-Grade Students

**Main findings**

• 12 third graders played a coin-tossing "Frog Race" game over 4 online lessons.
• They moved from predicting, to noticing that each game came out differently, to seeing that the blue frog (one head, one tail) usually wins.
• Many started making predictions about future games using words like "more likely", without any calculations.
• Some still chose by personal preference (e.g. favourite colour), and over half struggled to say how sure they were.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Predict → collect → compare → rate how likely → predict again is a great activity flow for us.
• Simple games with kids' own data work well, with no calculations needed.
• We could add a sample-size step and a simple confidence scale (impossible to certain).

**Reference**

Estrella, S., Méndez-Reina, M., & Vidal-Szabó, P. (2023). Exploring informal statistical inference in early statistics: A learning trajectory for third-grade students. Statistics Education Research Journal, 22(2), Article 10. https://doi.org/10.52041/serj.v22i2.426

## Paper 20: Review of Graph Comprehension Research: Implications for Instruction

**Main findings**

• Graph reading depends on the graph design, what you know about graphs, and what you know about the topic.
• Line graphs for trends, bar graphs for comparing, pie charts for parts of a whole.
• Label things directly instead of using a legend, especially for kids.
• Young kids often read graphs as pictures (e.g. speed graph = racetrack), but a little teaching helps.
• People tend to see what they expect in the data.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Clear rules for DataScope's charts (right chart type, direct labels, simple colours).
• Let kids switch chart types for the same data.
• DataScope can explore how kids build and interact with their own graphs.

**Reference**

Shah, P., & Hoeffner, J. (2002). Review of graph comprehension research: Implications for instruction. Educational Psychology Review, 14(1), 47–69.

## Paper 21: Graph/Chart Interpretation and Reading Comprehension as Critical Thinking Skills

**Main findings**

• 350 students took the Greek version of a critical thinking test.
• Primary (grade 6) and secondary students both scored a median of just 3 out of 9 on graph questions, and university students 7.
• Reading comprehension was similarly low for school students.
• Even a third of university students got a fire evacuation diagram question wrong.
• The authors link weak graph reading to weak critical thinking.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Don't assume kids can read charts: teach and scaffold it.
• Ask kids to explain and question charts, not just read values.
• Give teachers ready-made graph-reading support.

**Reference**

Malamitsa, K., Kokkotas, P., & Kasoutas, M. (2008). Graph/chart interpretation and reading comprehension as critical thinking skills. Science Education International, 19(4), 371–384.
Link: not added yet

## Paper 23: Inquiry-Based Learning in Primary School Mathematics: A Systematic Review of Its Impact on Student Learning Outcomes

**Main findings**

• Review of 20 study summaries (2017–2024) found through Google Scholar.
• Almost all found inquiry learning helped with understanding concepts, problem solving, critical thinking and motivation.
• Guided inquiry works better for younger kids, and free inquiry for older or more confident ones.
• The teacher acting as a guide is key. Time, materials and training are the main challenges.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Supports building DataScope around kids asking questions and investigating data.
• Start with guided inquiry for young kids, then open it up.
• Include teacher guidance and teaching aids.
• Better as background support than strong evidence.

**Reference**

Ashari, N. W. (year not given in the PDF). Inquiry-based learning in primary school mathematics: A systematic review of its impact on student learning outcomes. Pedagogy, 10(2), 559–571.
Link: not added yet

## Paper 24: The Value of Inquiry-Based Science Instruction for Elementary Students

**Main findings**

• Results were mixed: many studies showed better content, process skills and attitudes, but some showed no effect.
• Even kindergarteners understood tricky ideas (air, energy, heat) through guided inquiry.
• Science kits or free exploration alone didn't help much. Teacher guidance and training made the difference.
• Class discussion and mixing reading/writing with science helped kids explain and justify ideas.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Guided inquiry, not free play: build prompts and feedback into DataScope.
• Use "I think ___ because…" to help kids explain their data.
• Add quick check-ins during activities.

**Reference**

Enger, J. R. (2017). The value of inquiry-based science instruction for elementary students [Graduate research paper, University of Northern Iowa]. UNI ScholarWorks, Graduate Research Papers, 621. https://scholarworks.uni.edu/grp/621

## Paper 25: Transforming Primary Education: Balancing Social Skills and Academic Achievement through Global Inquiry-Based Learning Models

**Main findings**

• Literature review (using NVivo) of IBL in Finland, Australia, Singapore and the US.
• IBL improved collaboration, communication and empathy, plus engagement and results.
• Worked best in flexible systems (Finland, Australia) and less in test-driven ones (Singapore, US).
• Trained teachers and good technology were the biggest helpers. Lack of time, resources and training were the main barriers.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Supports DataScope's question → collect → analyse → conclude flow.
• Add group work and discussion around data.
• Include teacher support so it fits easily into lessons.

**Reference**

Seprie, Wuryandani, W., & Muthmainah. (2025). Transforming primary education: Balancing social skills and academic achievement through global inquiry-based learning models. Frontiers in Education, 10, 1512274. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2025.1512274

## Paper 26: KiData: A Simple Data Visualization Tool for Future Data Scientists

**Main findings**

• No results yet, it's a description of the KiData website.
• 12 lessons split into grades 1–2, 3–4 and 5–6, with a quiz under each one.
• Right answers turn green, wrong ones red, and kids can keep trying.
• Uses bright colours and hand-drawn cartoons to keep kids interested.
• Testing with kids, parents and teachers is planned for later.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Closest tool to DataScope, good to compare against.
• Borrow: grade-based lessons, cartoons, instant feedback with retries.
• DataScope can go further with hands-on data activities and real testing with kids.

**Reference**

Hamouda, S., Kancharla, S., Singh, G., Yang, L., Wang, Z., Zhang, S., Nirjhar, R., & Golden, J. (2023). KiData: Simple data visualization tool for future data scientists. Frontiers in Computer Science, 5, 1209515. https://doi.org/10.3389/fcomp.2023.1209515

## Paper 28: Making Conventional Data Collection More Child-Friendly: Questionnaires with Young Students

**Main findings**

• 286 Tanzanian primary kids answered in groups of 4–5 with a helper, and every group answered (100%).
• Most groups found active methods like games and group work helpful, and 94% disliked working alone.
• The "why" answers gave real insight into what kids value.
• Some questions failed: a confusing picture, a leading word ("good"), and a question too general with no timeframe.
• Works if questions fit kids' skills and culture, mean something to them, and kids can talk freely.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Good checklist for asking kids questions in DataScope: short, clear, no "not", a set timeframe, no leading words.
• Test pictures first and keep them simple.
• Group answering with a helper made it fun and got everyone to take part.
• Yes/no + "why" is a nice model for kids' own class surveys.

**Reference**

Mari, A. (2024). Making conventional data collection more Child-friendly: Questionnaires with young students. African Evaluation Journal, 12(1), a690. https://doi.org/10.4102/aej.v12i1.690

## Paper 29: Cooperative Inquiry: Developing New Technologies for Children with Children (Druin)

**Main findings**

• Describes "cooperative inquiry": kids and adults work as one design team over a long time.
• 3 techniques: watching kids in their own setting, designing together with paper, crayons and clay, and letting kids use lots of technology for days.
• Kids aged 7–10 were the best design partners.
• Tips: don't use video (kids freeze or perform), let kids take notes with drawings, and have one adult just chat with the kids.
• Led to KidPad and PETS, and both kids and adults said they learned a lot.

**How it helps DataScope**

• A method to design DataScope WITH kids, not just test on them.
• The 7–10 age range fits our target users.
• Useful tips for running kid sessions (interactor, no video, drawing notes).

**Reference**

Druin, A. (1999). Cooperative inquiry: Developing new technologies for children with children. In CHI '99: Human Factors in Computing Systems (pp. 592–599). Pittsburgh, PA, USA: ACM.

## Paper 30: The Effectiveness of Gamification in Teaching and Learning Mathematics: A Systematic Literature Review

**Main findings**

• Review of 22 studies (2018–2023) from Scopus and Web of Science.
• 17 used digital games, 3 non-digital games, and 2 looked at teachers' views.
• Gamification helped with engagement, motivation, remembering, performance, social skills, thinking skills and reducing maths anxiety.
• Works best when games are built around learning goals, since too many games can distract.

**How it helps DataScope**

• Supports adding points, badges, levels and instant feedback to DataScope.
• Self-paced challenges and team games could fit well.
• Keep games tied to the learning goal.
• Gap: few gamified tools for kids' data learning, so DataScope can fill it.

**Reference**

Jutin, N. T., & Maat, S. M. (2024). The effectiveness of gamification in teaching and learning mathematics: A systematic literature review. International Journal of Academic Research in Progressive Education and Development, 13(1), 1290–1309. http://dx.doi.org/10.6007/IJARPED/v13-i1/20703
